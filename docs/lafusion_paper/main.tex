\begin{abstract}
Multi-robot systems combining odometry with external visual detection
often rely on a discrete fallback — trusting detection when available,
odometry otherwise — which discards uncertainty information and offers
no principled behavior during long detection gaps. We replace this
fallback with a per-robot Extended Kalman Filter fusing odometry and
YOLO-based visual detection from a fixed overhead camera, validated in
two stages (synthetic NEES/NIS consistency, then real recorded data) on
a ROS2/Gazebo digital twin using three TurtleBot3 robots. Measured at
correction instants, the filter reduces position error by $+23.7\%$
relative to odometry alone under aggressive drift. However, measured
continuously — at every odometry reading — the same filter is $12.7\%$
\emph{worse} than raw odometry, under the sparse visual coverage
(0--28\%) our camera setup exhibits in practice. We show this reversal
is the expected consequence of operating below a critical observation
rate, not a design flaw, and connect it mechanistically to a covariance
ceiling required to keep Mahalanobis-gated association stable. We argue
that reporting both metrics, rather than only the commonly reported
correction-instant gain, is necessary to honestly characterize EKF
fusion under sparse observation, and discuss why alternative filters
(UKF, particle filter, IMM) do not resolve the underlying limitation.

\keywords{Extended Kalman Filter \and Sensor Fusion \and Multi-Robot Localization \and Sparse Observations \and Digital Twin}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multi-robot systems that combine onboard odometry with external visual
detection face a recurring design choice: how to combine two position
estimates that are individually noisy, arrive at different rates, and
are not always both available at the same instant. A common and
pragmatic solution — used, for instance, in the multi-robot digital
twin studied in this work prior to our contribution — is a \emph{discrete fallback}: use the
visual detection when one is confidently associated to a robot, and fall
back to raw odometry otherwise. This is simple and robust, but it
discards information: it does not let a low-confidence detection
partially correct odometry, does not propagate uncertainty from either
source into the estimate, and provides no principled way to reason about
what happens during long stretches without any visual detection at all.

Extended Kalman Filtering (EKF) is the classical answer to this class of
problem, offering a full probabilistic treatment of prediction and
correction with explicit uncertainty propagation. Its convergence
properties, however, are classically established under the assumption of
reasonably dense, regular observation~\cite{dissanayake2001slam} — an
assumption that a single fixed overhead camera, subject to a finite
field of view and self-occlusion during robot maneuvers, does not
generally satisfy. Whether and how this matters in practice, on a real
robotic platform rather than only in simulation, is the question this
paper investigates.

We replace this discrete fallback with a per-robot EKF fusing
odometry and YOLO-based visual detection from an overhead camera, and
evaluate it end-to-end on a ROS2/Gazebo digital twin using three
TurtleBot3 robots. Our contribution is twofold. First, we validate the
filter in two stages — synthetic NEES/NIS consistency, then real
recorded data — and report a real fusion gain ($+23.7\%$ position error
reduction relative to odometry alone, under aggressive drift, measured
at the instants a visual correction occurs). Second, and more centrally,
we show that the same filter, measured continuously rather than only at
correction instants, is $12.7\%$ \emph{worse} than raw odometry under
the sparse visual coverage (0--28\%) our overhead-camera setup actually
exhibits — and we explain this reversal mechanistically, connecting it
to established theory on Kalman filtering under intermittent
observations~\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent,censi2009geometric}. We
argue that reporting both metrics, rather than the more commonly
reported correction-instant gain alone, is necessary for an honest
characterization of EKF fusion in this regime, and we discuss why
alternative filters do not resolve the underlying limitation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Our work builds on three layers of prior literature: (i) the classical
formulation and convergence theory of EKF-based localization, (ii) the
theory of Kalman filtering under intermittent or sparse observations, and
(iii) recent multi-robot systems that fuse odometry with visual detection
in realistic simulation or physical testbeds.

\subsection{Classical EKF Localization and Convergence}

Bailey and Durrant-Whyte~\cite{bailey2006slam} provide the standard
tutorial formulation of EKF-based simultaneous localization and mapping,
formalizing the motion model $x_k = f(x_{k-1}, u_k) + w_k$ and observation
model $z_k = h(x_k) + v_k$ that our filter follows directly: odometry
drives prediction, and YOLO-based visual detections drive correction. We
adopt this notation throughout Section~\ref{sec:methodology}.

Dissanayake et al.~\cite{dissanayake2001slam} prove convergence properties
of EKF-SLAM covariance under the assumption of \emph{dense} landmark
observation — i.e., that the filter is corrected regularly and
predictably. This assumption is central to our Discussion
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}): our overhead-camera setup violates it by
design, since a robot is visually detected in only 0--28\% of odometry
readings depending on scenario, and this violation is precisely what
produces the continuous-error finding we report.

\subsection{Kalman Filtering Under Intermittent Observations}

Sinopoli et al.~\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent} establish that, when
observations arrive as a Bernoulli process, there exists a critical
arrival probability below which the expected estimation error covariance
diverges. Our Scenario~3 (curve/occlusion, 0\% visual correction over the
full recording) sits at the limiting case $p \to 0$ covered by this
theory — the covariance saturation we observe is therefore an expected
consequence of the observation regime, not a symptom of filter
misconfiguration.

Censi~\cite{censi2009geometric} revisits Sinopoli et al.'s result and
shows that the existence and value of this critical threshold depend on
the error metric used to summarize the covariance matrix (Euclidean vs.\
Riemannian manifold of SPD matrices). We report divergence using
$\mathrm{trace}(P)$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and note this
metric-dependence explicitly as a scoping remark rather than a claim of a
universal threshold.

\subsection{Recent Multi-Robot Visual-Odometric Fusion and Positioning}

Khosravi et al.~\cite{khosravi2026cooperative} report a decentralized
cooperative localization scheme for two robots, fusing local EKF
estimates with intermittent mutual LiDAR corrections (Gazebo and
physical hardware), with a $34$--$56\%$ RMSE reduction from fusion — the
same order of magnitude as our $+23.7\%$ correction-instant gain. Ahmed
and Fr\'emont~\cite{ahmed2026maritime} fuse a custom YOLOv8-RGBDis
detector with a per-target EKF for decentralized tracking of a
heterogeneous team (three UAVs, one surface vessel) in a maritime Gazebo
scenario, reporting $\mathrm{mAP@0.5}=0.995$ for their detector — close
to our own $0.995$ despite a different architecture and domain, a
coincidence we note but do not lean on. Neither work reports a
continuous-error regime in which fusion underperforms the unfused
baseline — the central, differentiating finding of our work. Dobrynin
and Garcia~\cite{dobrynin2025turtlebot} share our exact platform (three
TurtleBot3 units, ROS2) but solve camera-free relative localization, and
so do not encounter the sparse visual coverage regime we characterize;
we treat this as complementary work, revisited in
Section~\ref{sec:future-work}.

Our contribution is therefore the joint report of both the
correction-instant gain and the continuous-error degradation under
sparse coverage, together with a theoretical grounding
(\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent,censi2009geometric}) for why this
trade-off is expected rather than a bug in our implementation.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

Following the standard EKF formulation of Bailey and
Durrant-Whyte~\cite{bailey2006slam}, each robot is tracked independently
by a per-robot filter with state $x_k = [p_x, p_y, \theta]^\top$: 2D
position and heading. The motion model $f(\cdot)$ integrates the
\emph{delta} of consecutive odometry readings onto the current filter
state — not the absolute odometry value, which would silently discard any
prior visual correction. The observation model $h(\cdot)$ maps filter
state to the 2D position reported by a YOLO-based robot detector observing
the scene from a fixed overhead camera (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}).

\subsection{Prediction: Process Noise and Covariance Capping}

Process noise is a diagonal matrix $Q = \mathrm{diag}(0.5, 0.5, 0.25)$,
scaled by $\Delta t / 0.1$ at each prediction step to correct for the
real odometry rate ($\approx$29\,Hz, $\Delta t \approx 0.034$s) differing
from the 0.1s assumed during synthetic calibration
(Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}). $Q$ was calibrated empirically
against real recorded data, not only the synthetic NEES/NIS procedure: a
sweep from $10^{-6}$ to $1.0$ showed a monotonically increasing fusion
gain up to saturation between $Q=0.5$ and $Q=1.0$.

A hard ceiling is applied to the covariance matrix after each prediction,
$\mathrm{clip}(P, -\infty, 0.05)$, applied element-wise. Without this
ceiling, $P$ was observed to grow unbounded during long intervals without
visual correction (reaching $\mathrm{trace}(P) \approx 85$ in one recorded
event, against an expected $\mathrm{trace}(P) \approx 0.5$), which in turn
breaks Mahalanobis-gated association (Section~\ref{sec:association}): an
inflated covariance widens a robot's acceptance region enough to
incorrectly claim a neighboring robot's detection. This capping strategy
is functionally analogous to \emph{track coasting} in classical
multi-target tracking (Bar-Shalom), and is theoretically motivated by
Sinopoli et al.'s~\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent} result that covariance
diverges below a critical observation arrival rate — capping trades
strict optimality for bounded, predictable behavior under exactly the
sparse-observation regime we operate in (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We
report the covariance ceiling's effect on continuous-time estimation
error explicitly, rather than treating it as an implementation detail, in
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Correction: Confidence-Adaptive Measurement Noise}

Measurement noise $R$ is not fixed: it is derived linearly from the
YOLO detection confidence $c \in [0,1]$ (the third component of each
detected pose, repurposed as a confidence channel),
$\sigma = R_{\min} + (1-c)(R_{\max} - R_{\min})$, with
$R_{\min}=0.02$, $R_{\max}=0.20$ (meters), and $R = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2,
\sigma^2)$. A high-confidence detection is trusted more than a
low-confidence one during the Kalman update, rather than treating all
detections as equally reliable.

\subsection{Data Association}
\label{sec:association}

Because the overhead camera observes up to three robots simultaneously,
each incoming detection must be assigned to the correct robot before
correction. We use Mahalanobis-gated nearest-neighbor association,
computing $d^2 = e^\top P_{xy}^{-1} e$ for each (robot, detection) pair
using each robot's own 2$\times$2 position covariance submatrix $P_{xy}$,
and accepting the closest match only if $d^2 \le 9.21$ (the 99\%
confidence threshold of the $\chi^2$ distribution with 2 degrees of
freedom). This is stricter than raw Euclidean nearest-neighbor and
accounts for each robot's individual uncertainty, but inherits a known
limitation of covariance-based gating discussed by Altendorfer and
Wirkert~\cite{altendorfer2016association}: an inflated covariance widens
a track's acceptance region, which can bias association toward
lower-confidence or more uncertain tracks — precisely the failure mode
that motivated the covariance ceiling described above.

\subsection{Camera Calibration}

Camera intrinsics are obtained via \texttt{cv2.calibrateCamera}
following Zhang's method~\cite{zhang2000calibration}, using a
synthetically generated checkerboard spawned at multiple poses in the
simulated scene. Reprojection error alone was found to be an unreliable
calibration-quality signal: an initial calibration using five poses
concentrated near the frame center achieved a deceptively low
reprojection error (0.018px) while yielding a focal length 8.9$\times$
larger than the value expected from the camera's known field of view —
classical overfitting from an ill-conditioned, poorly distributed pose
set. We therefore add an explicit sanity check comparing calibrated
$f_x$ against the FOV-derived theoretical value, accepting only
calibrations within a $0.5\times$--$2.0\times$ ratio. The accepted
calibration (four well-distributed poses) achieves $f_x=739.4$,
$f_y=733.6$ ($1.33\times$ the theoretical ratio) with a mean
reprojection error of 0.162px, within the 0--0.2px range considered good
practice~\cite{zhang2000calibration}.

\subsection{Two-Stage Filter Validation}

Before evaluating fusion gain, we validate that the filter itself is
correctly calibrated, in two independent stages:

\textbf{(1) Synthetic validation via NEES/NIS.} Following Bar-Shalom and
Chen's methodology, we generate a synthetic trajectory with known ground
truth and run 30 independent Monte Carlo seeds (2000 steps each,
58{,}500 post-warmup samples). The filter achieves NEES $=2.955$
(expected $3.0$, 1.5\% relative error) and NIS $=1.982$ (expected $2.0$,
0.9\% relative error) — close enough to nominal values to be considered
well-calibrated in practice, even though the formal $\chi^2$ 95\% bounds
are too narrow to pass at this sample size (an expected effect of
aggregating tens of thousands of samples, not a sign of miscalibration).

\textbf{(2) Real-data validation.} The calibrated filter is then run
against odometry and YOLO detections recorded from the live digital twin
(Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}), where — unlike the synthetic
case — observation coverage is sparse and irregular by construction of
the physical scenario, not by design choice.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

We evaluate our EKF within a ROS2/Gazebo digital twin of a
multi-robot warehouse-style environment. Three TurtleBot3 Waffle units
(\texttt{robot1}--\texttt{robot3}) operate in a shared simulated world
alongside a static overhead camera mounted at $(0,0,3)$\,m with
$90^\circ$ downward pitch and a $60^\circ$ horizontal field of view
(1.047\,rad), publishing $640\times480$ RGB frames at 10\,Hz. Each robot
also publishes its own odometry (\texttt{/robotN/odom}) at
$\approx$29\,Hz and a 2D LiDAR scan (\texttt{/robotN/scan}, unused in
this work — see Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\subsection{Visual Detection}

Robot detection is performed by a YOLO model trained on 375 manually
annotated frames captured from the same overhead camera setup, achieving
$\mathrm{mAP@0.5}=0.995$ on the held-out validation split (image size
416px). At inference, the detector publishes a \texttt{PoseArray} on
\texttt{/robot\_detections}, repurposing the otherwise-unused $z$
component of each pose to carry the YOLO confidence score, so the EKF
correction step (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) can consume it directly.

\subsection{Recorded Scenarios}

Three scenarios were recorded as ROS2 bags (MCAP format,
$\sim$18\,s each, $\sim$2\,MB each), covering distinct motion and
occlusion regimes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Stationary} (\texttt{cenario1\_parado}): robots remain at
    their spawn poses. 152 detection messages against 550 odometry
    readings per robot — the highest visual coverage of the three
    scenarios ($\approx$28\%).
  \item \textbf{Straight motion} (\texttt{cenario2\_reto}): robots move
    forward at $v_x=0.1$\,m/s. 144 detection messages against 522
    odometry readings per robot ($\approx$11\% coverage); ground-truth
    \texttt{robot1} displacement of $\sim$1.5\,m confirms real motion,
    not merely a commanded velocity with no physical effect.
  \item \textbf{Curve / occlusion} (\texttt{cenario3\_curva}): robots
    move with $v_x=0.12$\,m/s and $\omega_z=0.4$\,rad/s. 145 detection
    messages recorded on the topic, but \textbf{zero} of them were
    successfully associated to \texttt{robot1} by the gating procedure
    of Section~\ref{sec:association} over the full $\sim$18\,s recording
    — the single fixed overhead viewpoint loses robots to
    self-occlusion and out-of-frame motion during turning, producing the
    0\% coverage case discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
\end{itemize}

Timestamp synchronization between \texttt{/robot\_detections} and
odometry was verified directly via \texttt{rosbag2\_py} in all three
scenarios: message-arrival windows coincide within each recording, with
no gap exceeding 500\,ms on any channel — ruling out sensor-clock
desynchronization, a known confound in multi-sensor fusion pipelines, as
an explanation for the results reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Reproducibility Package}

To support independent verification, we release a package containing the
Gazebo world file, robot URDF (including the unused LiDAR, for
completeness), camera calibration parameters, all EKF
hyperparameters, and the git commit SHA corresponding to the exact code
used to produce the results in Section~\ref{sec:results}. We explicitly
document one non-obvious dependency: \texttt{ros-humble-rosbag2-storage-}
\texttt{mcap} is not installed by default on ROS2 Humble and must be
installed separately to read the released bags.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report two distinct error metrics, deliberately kept separate rather
than collapsed into a single number: \emph{correction-instant error},
measured only at the moments a YOLO detection is successfully associated
and fused (the metric most commonly reported in related
work — Section~\ref{sec:related-work}), and \emph{continuous error},
measured at every odometry reading regardless of whether a correction
occurred. As we show below, these two metrics disagree in sign, and both
are necessary to honestly characterize the filter's behavior under
sparse observation.

\subsection{Filter Calibration}

The synthetic NEES/NIS validation described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} confirms the filter is well-calibrated
before any real-data evaluation. Fig.~\ref{fig:nees-nis} shows both
statistics remaining close to their theoretical expected values
(NEES$\to 3.0$, NIS$\to 2.0$) across the Monte Carlo run.

\subsection{Correction-Instant Error}

Table~\ref{tab:correction-error} reports position error under three
injected odometry drift regimes (no drift, light drift, aggressive
drift), aggregated across all three recorded scenarios ($n=1323$). Under
aggressive drift — the regime in which raw odometry has already
accumulated substantial error before a visual correction becomes
available — the EKF reduces mean position error by $+23.7\%$ relative to
odometry alone (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p<0.001$). Under light drift, the
gain is negative ($-5.3\%$): visual detection error ($\approx$3--6\,cm)
is not yet smaller than the still-small odometry error, so fusion offers

filter, not a failure.

\subsection{Continuous Error: The Central Finding}

When error is instead measured at \emph{every} odometry reading — not
only at correction instants — the result reverses. Table~\ref{tab:continuous-error}
reports continuous-time error under aggressive drift, using the same
recordings as Table~\ref{tab:correction-error}. Aggregated across all
readings ($n=1607$), the EKF is $12.7\%$ \emph{worse} than raw odometry
(Wilcoxon signed-rank, $p<0.001$) — the opposite sign from the
correction-instant result.

Two observations explain this reversal. First, the degradation scales
inversely with YOLO coverage: Scenario 3 (0\% coverage — no successful
visual correction over the entire $\sim$18\,s recording) shows a change
of exactly $0.0\%$, because with zero corrections the EKF's prediction
step degenerates to pure odometry-delta integration, making it
mathematically identical to the unfused baseline. Scenarios 1 and 2
(27.6\% and 11.3\% coverage) show similar $\sim$19\% degradation despite
their different coverage levels, suggesting the effect saturates quickly
once corrections become sparse enough for the covariance ceiling
(Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) to bind.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{../lafusion/figures/lafusion_covariance_trace.png}

Second, and mechanistically explaining the first, Fig.~\ref{fig:covariance-trace}
shows why: $\mathrm{trace}(P)$ saturates at the covariance ceiling almost
immediately after any gap without correction, and only drops when a
correction lands. In Scenario 3, the trace saturates within the first
observed step and never drops again for the remainder of the recording.
This is precisely the divergence regime predicted by Sinopoli et
al.~\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent} for observation arrival rates below a
critical threshold: the filter's covariance ceiling prevents unbounded
growth (and the association failures it would otherwise cause,
Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), but it does so by capping the filter's
\emph{own estimate of its uncertainty}, not by making its state estimate
actually more accurate during the uncorrected interval — the filter
remains overconfident in its odometry-only prediction relative to how
uncertain that prediction actually is.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The Continuous-Error Finding Is Expected, Not a Bug}

The natural reading of Table~\ref{tab:continuous-error} is unflattering:
our EKF, evaluated continuously, loses to the odometry it is supposed to
improve upon. We argue this is the expected consequence of operating
below the critical observation rate identified by Sinopoli et
al.~\cite{sinopoli2004intermittent}, not evidence of a flawed design.
Dissanayake et al.'s~\cite{dissanayake2001slam} classical convergence
proof for EKF-based localization assumes regular, dense observation of
landmarks; our overhead-camera setup violates that assumption by
construction (0--28\% coverage across scenarios), and the theory
predicts exactly the covariance divergence we would see without a
ceiling, and the practical consequence we do see — an
overconfident filter — with one. Framed this way, Scenario 3's $0.0\%$
continuous-error change is not a null result but a clean empirical
confirmation: with zero corrections, our filter's prediction step is
provably identical to odometry-delta integration, and the measured
change is exactly zero, as the mechanism predicts it should be.

We follow Censi's~\cite{censi2009geometric} caution that the existence
and value of a critical threshold depends on the covariance summary
metric used; we report $\mathrm{trace}(P)$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:covariance-trace}) as a scoping choice, not a claim that
divergence is metric-independent.

\subsection{Why EKF, and Why Covariance Capping}

A natural question is whether an alternative filter would avoid this
trade-off. We argue no standard Bayesian filter resolves the
zero-coverage case (Scenario 3): a particle filter degenerates to pure
process propagation with no likelihood to reweight against, an Unscented
Kalman Filter targets observation-model nonlinearity rather than
observation \emph{rate} (our near-linear overhead projection gains
little from it), and Interacting Multiple Model filters still require
some observation to weight competing modes. We therefore treat the low
coverage itself — a single fixed camera viewpoint subject to occlusion
during turns — as the root cause, consistent with sensor-placement
literature establishing that estimation limits under a given sensor
topology hold independently of the filter processing its observations.
The covariance ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}) is, in this
light, an engineering approximation of \emph{track coasting} in
classical multi-target tracking, trading strict statistical optimality
for association stability under this sparse regime — not an ad hoc
workaround.

\subsection{Mahalanobis Gating Under Saturated Covariance}

The gating strategy of Section~\ref{sec:association} inherits the
limitation discussed by Altendorfer and
Wirkert~\cite{altendorfer2016association}: once covariance saturates at
the ceiling, the gate's acceptance region is at its widest and most
permissive for the longest stretches of the recording — precisely when
the filter's true uncertainty is least well characterized by a fixed
ceiling value. We did not observe incorrect cross-robot association in
the three recorded scenarios, but this is a property of our specific
robot spacing and motion patterns, not a guarantee that holds in denser
multi-robot configurations.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The continuous-error finding of Section~\ref{sec:results} points to
concrete directions rather than closing the question. \textbf{Adaptive
process noise.} Instead of a fixed covariance ceiling, $Q$ could be scaled
as a function of time since the last successful correction, allowing
$P$ to reflect growing uncertainty during long gaps rather than
saturating at a fixed value — a more principled alternative to
covariance capping, at the cost of an additional hyperparameter to
calibrate. \textbf{LiDAR as a third fusion source.} Each robot in our
testbed already publishes 2D LiDAR scans that go unused in this
work; fusing mutual LiDAR-based relative range, in the spirit of
Khosravi et al.~\cite{khosravi2026cooperative}, could supply corrections
during the exact intervals where the overhead camera loses visual
contact, directly targeting the zero-coverage regime of
Scenario~3. \textbf{Formal projection uncertainty.} Our observation
model currently treats each YOLO detection's 2D position as exact up to
the confidence-derived $R$; propagating the full projection Jacobian
uncertainty (camera calibration error, ground-plane assumption) into $R$
would make the confidence-to-noise mapping more principled.
\textbf{Combining with distributed relative localization.} Dobrynin and
Garcia's~\cite{dobrynin2025turtlebot} camera-free distributed Kalman
filtering on the same TurtleBot3 platform is complementary rather than
competing: combining robot-to-robot relative localization with our
overhead-camera fusion could provide a second, independent correction
channel structurally different from — and available during gaps
in — visual coverage.

\section{Conclusion}

We replaced a discrete visual/odometry fallback in a multi-robot digital
twin with an Extended Kalman Filter, validated in two stages and
evaluated on real recorded data from three TurtleBot3 robots. The filter
delivers a genuine, statistically significant gain at correction
instants ($+23.7\%$), but is measurably worse than raw odometry when
evaluated continuously ($-12.7\%$) under the sparse visual coverage
(0--28\%) our fixed overhead camera exhibits in practice — including a
zero-coverage scenario where the filter's behavior collapses, exactly as
expected, to the unfused baseline. Rather than report only the favorable
metric, we characterize both and ground the reversal in established
theory on Kalman filtering under intermittent observations. EKF fusion
remains the right tool for this problem, but its correctness under
sparse observation must be measured and reported explicitly — not
assumed from a single favorable number — and the low observation rate
itself, not the choice of filter, is the binding constraint future work
should target.
